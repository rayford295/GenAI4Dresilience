# GenAI Across the Disaster Management Lifecycle

_Text from the chapter **Generative AI for Disaster Resilience** (Yang, Zou, Tian, Zhou, Abedin, Li, Tu), submitted to *Geography in the Age of Generative AI: Innovations in Mapping, Analysis & Geospatial Applications* (CRC Press / Taylor & Francis). Back to [README](../README.md)._

As illustrated in Figure 1, this chapter proposes a lifecycle framework that places GenAI as a cross-cutting analytical and decision-support layer that combines generation, multimodal understanding, and reasoning with heterogeneous geospatial data across the four phases of disaster management. In mitigation, GenAI can integrate environmental, infrastructural, and social information to construct plausible risk scenarios and compare options for reducing exposure and vulnerability. During preparedness, it can synthesize rapidly changing observations to improve situational awareness and inform early warnings, evacuation strategies, and resource plans. During response, it can interpret incomplete multimodal evidence to assess damage and needs, identify priority areas, and support the coordination of time-sensitive emergency actions. In recovery, it can compare alternative rebuilding and adaptation pathways, examine long-term trade-offs and uneven community needs, and support participatory planning. Multi-agent orchestration can coordinate these specialized functions, while human oversight and validation remain essential throughout the lifecycle. The four phases form a connected cycle in which information generated during response and recovery can strengthen future mitigation and preparedness. Building on this framework, the rest of this section elaborates on how GenAI’ multimodal, generative, reasoning, and multi-agent capacities can support key disaster-resilience tasks in each phase.

### 2.1 Mitigation: Risk Identification and Vulnerability Reduction

Mitigation planning needs to estimate where and why losses may occur before a disaster, when damage is not observable and both hazards and exposed communities may change over time (Raikes et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2025). This requires integrating information about terrain and elevation, land use, building footprints, drainage networks, critical facilities, infrastructure conditions, historical hazards, and population characteristics. (Liu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). However, historical event archives systematically underrepresent rare, compound, and cascading hazards and may not reflect future climate, development, or infrastructure conditions. Models trained on observed losses will structurally underestimate extreme but plausible risks (Cai, Lam, Zou, et al., 2018). Risks also emerge from nonlinear and place-specific interactions among physical exposure, terrain features, infrastructure interdependence, building condition, social vulnerability, and environmental buffers. The interpretive value of composite risk scores derived from weighted map overlays is limited (Qiang et al., 2023; Singh & Jeganathan, 2025). For example, a high-vulnerability neighborhood protected by an aging or poorly maintained levee faces a different risk than a similar neighborhood without that infrastructure dependency, even if both receive similar scores from conventional composite scoring approaches (Lei et al., 2025; Tay et al., 2022). Consequently, standard hazard maps and vulnerability indices may indicate where risk is concentrated without adequately explaining how it is produced or which interventions could reduce it.

The most distinctive value of GenAI is not replacing traditional risk assessment models. Instead, generative models can connect heterogeneous evidence, explore counterfactual scenarios, and explain the consequences of alternative interventions. For instance, a multimodal system could combine flood-model outputs with elevation data, building footprints, drainage networks, critical-facility locations, maintenance records, and demographic information to identify neighborhoods where projected inundation coincides with aging infrastructure, limited mobility, or dependence on vulnerable services. Generative capabilities could then organize alternative scenarios involving more intense rainfall, new floodplain development, levee failure, drainage upgrades, or building elevation, using outputs from validated hazard and infrastructure models. Reasoning models could compare these scenarios, explain how different risk drivers influence expected impacts, and identify mitigation options that reduce losses across population groups. Multi-agent workflows could further compare the explicitly defined objectives and constraints of residents, planners, utilities, and insurers, revealing potential trade-offs and conflicts among mitigation alternatives. The resulting products could include scenario-based risk maps, transparent summaries of major risk drivers, and mitigation alternatives for expert and stakeholder review.

These applications require clear methodological and operational boundaries. Generative models can produce scenarios that are internally consistent but physically implausible without adequate domain grounding and empirical validation, and synthetic hazard footprints can be mistaken for forecasts if provenance is not made explicit. Errors and biases in building inventories, demographic data, or planning documents can also propagate into risk explanations and recommended investments, while fluent natural-language rationales may communicate greater certainty than the evidence supports. Similarly, role-based agents cannot substitute for direct stakeholder participation and may misrepresent community preferences or institutional constraints. Mitigation workflows should therefore preserve data provenance, distinguish observed and modeled evidence from synthetic scenarios, communicate uncertainty, and require expert and stakeholder validation before outputs inform decisions. These safeguards are important because mitigation products provide baseline evidence that preparedness and response systems depend on. Errors introduced at this stage can propagate throughout the disaster management lifecycle.

### 2.2 Preparedness: Monitoring, Early Warning, and Anticipatory Planning

The preparedness phase involves tasks that interpret signals of future disasters that are imminent and accelerating. The core challenge is not data integration, which is tractable, but the political economy of false alarms. The asymmetric costs of making warning decisions are difficult to resolve using technical improvements alone (Guo et al., 2025; Lamberti-Castronuovo et al., 2022). Issuing an evacuation order that is later proven unnecessary erodes institutional credibility and public compliance with future warnings. Contrarily, failing to issue one in time produces casualties. These asymmetries vary across communities, hazard types, and institutional contexts. The value of a preparedness-oriented monitoring system is not only determined by its detection accuracy alone but also by whether its outputs can be translated into actionable decisions by specific actors within available time windows.

The data environment of preparedness amplifies this difficulty. Dynamic streams from satellite imagery, SAR products, UAV observations, sensor networks, meteorological forecasts, emergency calls, and social media each carry different latencies, spatial resolutions, failure modes, and credibility characteristics (Chandran & Vipin, 2024; Sathianarayanan & Hsu, 2025). A high-confidence satellite overpass may arrive hours after a flood wave has already peaked; a social media report may surface structural flooding before any sensor confirms it but requires verification before it can trigger a public alert. Integrating these streams is therefore a semantic and temporal problem: deciding which evidence to trust, when to act on partial signals, and how to communicate uncertainty to decision-makers who are operating under pressure (Z. Li et al., 2025). Existing monitoring systems often treat these questions separately, with image-processing pipelines, weather models, and alert systems designed independently and connected through manual handoffs.

GenAI can contribute to preparedness by maintaining a coherent situation model that connects heterogeneous evidence streams and translates them into decision-relevant terms. Rather than treating each data source as an independent input to a detection algorithm, multimodal understanding can cross-reference weak signals across imagery, sensor readings, call-center transcripts, and field reports, raising confidence when multiple sources converge and flagging contradictions that warrant manual review. Generative models can fill bounded data gaps, simulating plausible hazard trajectories during cloud cover or sensor outages, and produce synthetic event sequences for stress-testing warning thresholds and evacuation protocols before an actual emergency. Reasoning models can present forecasts with explicit uncertainty ranges, identify which assumptions are driving the range, and explain the needed additional information to reduce uncertainty. This structured communication is more effective for emergency managers than  probability-based maps alone in time-sensitive decision-makings (Mahdi et al., 2023; Reichstein et al., 2025). Multi-agent frameworks can rehearse the interactions among transportation agencies, hospitals, utilities, schools, and residential populations under different hazard trajectories, revealing coordination failures and resource conflicts before they occur.

The primary operational requirement is auditability under time pressure. Each GenAI-supported alert or recommendation should display its timestamp and geographic scope, the provenance and freshness of its supporting sources, corroborating and conflicting evidence, and the uncertainty inherited from upstream models. The system must clearly distinguish direct observations, forecasts generated by domain models, and GenAI-generated inferences or scenarios. Agencies should also establish decision protocols specifying the evidence thresholds for moving from monitoring to an advisory, warning, or evacuation recommendation; who is authorized to act at each stage; when human review is mandatory; and how an output can be challenged or overridden. These thresholds should reflect the locally defined consequences of false and missed alarms and be developed with responsible agencies and affected communities. Human officials should remain accountable for issuing public warnings. Finally, alerts, overrides, and their supporting rationales should be logged and transferred into the response phase so that responders can distinguish what was directly observed, scientifically forecast, and inferred by GenAI.

### 2.3 Response: Rapid Damage Assessment and Emergency Coordination

Response compresses the analytical problems of the earlier phases into hours. Responders must simultaneously interpret multi-source, multimodal and incomplete evidence, update priorities as conditions evolve, and coordinate actions across agencies with different information, resources, and authorities, all before the situation stabilizes (Hu et al., 2023; Miller et al., 2025). The speed imperative can obscure a structural bias that is analytically more consequential: post-disaster evidence is not spatially neutral. Satellite and aerial imagery tend to cover accessible areas with denser infrastructure and greater image density and resolution, while the communities that suffer the most severe damage are often those with the least pre-existing data infrastructure, including informal settlements, rural areas, and neighborhoods with low property values and high deferred maintenance (Lozano et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2023). A damage assessment that relies primarily on remote sensing without correcting for this geographic selection will systematically underestimate needs in the places where response resources are most needed.

Cross-view evidence alignment compounds this difficulty. Satellites, UAVs, street-level cameras, and field reports observe the same physical damage at different scales, angles, and times, and a building that appears intact from orbit may be structurally compromised at grade (Zhai & Peng, 2020; Zheng et al., 2021). Integrating these views requires not just geometric registration but semantic interpretation: what does a pattern visible in one modality imply about what would be observed in another? Damage patterns also vary with building type, construction era, hazard mechanism, and local preparedness in ways that limit how well models trained on prior events transfer to new ones (Zou, Liao, et al., 2023). Emergency coordination introduces a further problem: most existing tools deliver maps and alerts but do not explain the evidence behind priorities or connect damage observations to the role-specific decisions that different organizations can make (Hu & Janowicz, 2015).

GenAI’s contribution to response is most defensible when it closes the gap between what is observed and what should be decided, and when it does so in terms that incident commanders can verify and override. Multimodal understanding can align satellite, aerial, and ground-level evidence to identify damaged structures, blocked road segments, flooded areas, and utility failures, especially when any single source is insufficient (Chandran & Vipin, 2024). Bounded generative reconstruction can plausibly complete sparse damage patterns and flag high-probability damage in unobserved areas, provided that synthetic outputs are clearly marked and separated from field-verified evidence. Reasoning models can produce severity assessments with supporting evidence chains and explicit confidence bounds, enabling responders to identify which assessments rest on strong multi-source confirmation and which depend on inference from partial data. Multi-agent frameworks can distribute response tasks across transportation, medical, utility, and public-safety agents, evaluating evacuation routing, search-and-rescue prioritization, supply logistics, and shelter operations as coupled rather than independent problems.

Safe operational use requires an evidence architecture that makes every assessment traceable and contestable. Each GenAI-supported output should identify its source, location, timestamp, and geographic coverage; distinguish among direct observations, corroborated reports, model-based estimates, and GenAI-generated inferences; and display relevant uncertainty, conflicting evidence, and data gaps. Systems should flag stale inputs, poorly observed areas, and conditions that differ substantially from their training data. Response agencies must also establish protocols specifying which recommendations require field verification, what evidence thresholds trigger escalation, who has authority to approve or override an action, and how overrides are documented. Life-critical or difficult-to-reverse decisions, such as directing search-and-rescue teams, declaring an evacuation route safe, or deprioritizing a neighborhood, should remain under accountable human authority. Because uneven observations can translate into inequitable resource allocation, agencies should continuously compare data coverage, assessment errors, and response decisions across communities. Finally, preserving the evidence, recommendations, decisions, and overrides associated with each action creates an auditable incident record and provides a more reliable baseline for subsequent recovery planning.

### 2.4 Recovery: Short-Term Restoration and Long-Term Adaptation

Recovery is the phase that directly tests whether disaster management improves conditions or reconstitutes vulnerability. Research consistently documents that reconstruction processes tend to reproduce pre-disaster settlement patterns, land uses, and social inequalities even when the evidence for change is abundant, not simply because decision-makers lack information but because the institutional incentives, legal frameworks, financing structures, and political pressures operating during recovery favor rapid restoration over risk-informed, long-term transformation (Berke et al., 2014; Blackman et al., 2017; Raikes et al., 2019). A neighborhood rebuilt quickly to pre-disaster specifications generates insurance recoveries, resumes tax revenues, and satisfies visible political demands; a neighborhood rebuilt more slowly to a safer standard generates none of these short-term returns and may relocate residents whose political voice shaped the recovery process in the first place. This is not primarily an analytical failure but an institutional one, and it frames what GenAI can and cannot contribute.

Within this institutional context, the analytical challenges of recovery are distinct from those of earlier phases. Monitoring must connect physical reconstruction (building permits, debris removal, and infrastructure repair) with functional recovery across housing, employment, public services, and community networks, on timescales of months to years rather than hours (Chelleri et al., 2015; Copeland et al., 2020; Finucane et al., 2020). These trajectories are uneven in ways that aggregate indicators hide: areas that recover quickly on average may contain households that do not recover at all, and the gap between average and marginal recovery is often widest along existing axes of social inequality (Janssen & Anderies, 2007). Reconstruction planning must simultaneously manage these near-term equity concerns and evaluate long-term risk reduction options (elevated structures, infrastructure hardening, managed retreat, and green buffer investment) under uncertain future climate, demographic, and economic conditions (Yang et al., 2025). These decisions involve trade-offs that cannot be resolved by analysis alone because they require choices among competing values: speed versus safety, local preference versus regional risk management, private property rights versus collective flood protection.

GenAI’s roles in recovery are expanding the range of alternatives that decision-makers can deliberate over, and making the assumptions behind different recovery pathways explicit and comparable. Multimodal understanding can track recovery indicators across satellite imagery, building permit databases, mobility traces, administrative records, and community-generated reports, providing a richer and more current picture of where recovery is lagging than administrative reporting alone (Blackman et al., 2017). Generative models can construct alternative rebuilding pathways, including rapid restoration, risk-informed in-place adaptation, selective relocation, and phased infrastructure hardening, with explicit assumptions about costs, timelines, displaced populations, and residual risk under different climate futures. Reasoning models can compare these scenarios by making visible the trade-offs among speed, equity, environmental impact, and long-term risk that are otherwise implicit in planning documents. Multi-agent frameworks can simulate negotiations among residents, planners, insurers, developers, and utility operators, surfacing the points of conflict and coordination failure that participatory planning processes must resolve.

Several safeguards are necessary for these capabilities to support equitable and accountable recovery. Recovery indicators should document their sources, temporal coverage, geographic scale, uncertainty, and missing populations so that apparent progress is not confused with incomplete observation. Data coverage and GenAI model performance should be evaluated across neighborhoods and population groups, because communities experiencing prolonged displacement or informal recovery are often the least visible in administrative and geospatial datasets. GenAI-supported scenarios should be constrained by validated domain models, available resources, regulatory requirements, and local implementation capacity, with generated assumptions clearly distinguished from observed evidence and model-based estimates. Scenario comparisons should also report how benefits, costs, displacement, and residual risks are distributed rather than relying only on community-wide averages. Furthermore, simulated stakeholder perspectives cannot substitute for meaningful participation by affected communities, and decisions about acceptable risk, relocation, and resource allocation should remain with accountable institutions and the people affected. By documenting which recovery strategies were adopted, who benefited, where recovery stalled, and how risks changed, these systems can feed evidence from recovery back into future vulnerability assessments, mitigation priorities, and preparedness plans, thereby closing the disaster management lifecycle.
